# Asthma

## Overview
Asthma is a heterogeneous disease, usually with chronic airway inflammation, defined by variable respiratory symptoms and variable expiratory airflow limitation.

## Typical presentation
- Wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough that vary over time and in intensity.
- Worse at night or on waking; triggered by exercise, allergens, cold air, viral infections or irritants.
- Personal or family history of allergy or eczema is common.

## Diagnostic criteria
Asthma requires a history of variable respiratory symptoms and confirmed variable expiratory airflow limitation. Reduced FEV1/FVC should be documented at least once when FEV1 is low, together with at least one of the following in adults:
- Positive bronchodilator reversibility: FEV1 increases by more than 12% and more than 200 mL from baseline.
- Excessive peak expiratory flow variability: average daily diurnal variability above 10% over 2 weeks.
- Significant improvement after 4 weeks of anti-inflammatory treatment: FEV1 increases by more than 12% and more than 200 mL.
- Positive bronchial challenge test.
- Excessive variation between visits: FEV1 changes by more than 12% and more than 200 mL.

In children, a bronchodilator increase of more than 12% of the predicted FEV1 is used.

## Confirmatory and supporting tests
- Spirometry with bronchodilator testing, ideally before controller treatment.
- Peak flow diary.
- Bronchial provocation testing with methacholine or exercise when spirometry is normal.
- FeNO supports type 2 airway inflammation but does not by itself confirm or exclude asthma.
- Blood eosinophils and allergy testing to characterize the phenotype.

## Differential diagnosis
- Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
- Inducible laryngeal obstruction.
- Bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis.
- Heart failure.
- Gastroesophageal reflux and post-nasal drip.
- Foreign body, particularly in children.

## Diagnostic pitfalls and red flags
- Spirometry can be normal between episodes, so repeat it during symptoms.
- Inhaled steroids can make airflow variability harder to demonstrate.
- Inability to complete sentences, silent chest, cyanosis, exhaustion or confusion indicate a severe or life-threatening attack.

## Guideline basis
Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) strategy report.
