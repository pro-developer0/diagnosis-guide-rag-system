# Typhoid Fever

## Overview
Typhoid fever is a systemic infection caused by Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi, spread through contaminated food and water. Paratyphoid fever is a clinically similar illness.

## Typical presentation
- Fever that climbs over several days, headache, malaise, abdominal pain, constipation or diarrhea and dry cough.
- Signs: relative bradycardia, coated tongue, hepatosplenomegaly and rose spots.
- Travel to or residence in an endemic area is an important clue.

## Diagnostic criteria
Confirmed typhoid fever requires isolation of Salmonella Typhi from a sterile site or clinical specimen, such as blood, bone marrow or stool, in a patient with compatible illness. Suspected cases are based on prolonged fever and exposure risk.

## Confirmatory and supporting tests
- Blood culture is the standard first test and has its best yield in the first week of illness.
- Bone marrow culture is the most sensitive culture and stays positive after antibiotics have started.
- Stool and urine cultures are more useful later and to detect carriers, but a positive result alone does not prove acute illness.
- Antimicrobial susceptibility testing of every isolate, because resistance is widespread.
- Complete blood count and liver enzymes may show mild anemia, leukopenia or raised transaminases.

## Differential diagnosis
- Malaria.
- Dengue.
- Tuberculosis.
- Brucellosis.
- Rickettsial infection.
- Viral hepatitis.
- Sepsis from another cause.

## Diagnostic pitfalls and red flags
- A negative blood culture does not exclude typhoid, particularly after antibiotics.
- The Widal test has limited sensitivity and specificity and should not be relied on alone.
- Rapid antibody tests have variable accuracy.
- Severe abdominal pain, peritonism, gastrointestinal bleeding or confusion, typically in the second to third week, suggests perforation, hemorrhage or encephalopathy.
