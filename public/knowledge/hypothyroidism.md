# Hypothyroidism

## Overview
Hypothyroidism is deficient thyroid hormone production. Primary disease of the thyroid is most common, and autoimmune (Hashimoto) thyroiditis is the leading cause where iodine intake is adequate.

## Typical presentation
- Fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, dry skin, hair loss, low mood, slowed thinking.
- Menstrual irregularity or infertility.
- Signs: bradycardia, delayed relaxation of tendon reflexes, goiter, facial puffiness, non-pitting edema.

## Diagnostic criteria
- Overt primary hypothyroidism: elevated TSH with low free T4.
- Subclinical hypothyroidism: elevated TSH with normal free T4.
- Central hypothyroidism: low free T4 with a low or inappropriately normal TSH.

Repeat TSH and free T4 after 2 to 3 months to confirm subclinical hypothyroidism, because mild elevations can normalize.

## Confirmatory and supporting tests
- TSH is the first-line test; add free T4 when TSH is abnormal or central disease is suspected.
- Anti-thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies support autoimmune thyroiditis.
- Lipid profile and complete blood count may show secondary changes.
- Thyroid ultrasound is reserved for a palpable nodule or structural abnormality, not routine diagnosis.
- Pituitary evaluation and MRI when central hypothyroidism is found.

## Differential diagnosis
- Non-thyroidal illness.
- Recovery phase of thyroiditis.
- Depression, sleep apnea, anemia and other causes of fatigue.
- Drug effects, including amiodarone, lithium and immune checkpoint inhibitors.

## Diagnostic pitfalls and red flags
- High-dose biotin can distort thyroid immunoassays.
- Use trimester-specific reference ranges in pregnancy.
- Avoid testing during acute severe illness unless thyroid disease is strongly suspected.
- Hypothermia, altered consciousness, bradycardia and hypoventilation suggest myxedema coma, a medical emergency.
