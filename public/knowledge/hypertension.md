# Hypertension

## Overview
Hypertension is sustained elevation of arterial blood pressure and a major risk factor for stroke, coronary disease, heart failure and chronic kidney disease. Most cases are primary.

## Typical presentation
- Usually no symptoms and found on routine measurement.
- Severe elevation may cause headache, visual change, chest pain, breathlessness or neurological symptoms.

## Diagnostic criteria
Base the diagnosis on the average of at least 2 properly taken readings on at least 2 separate occasions.
- American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association: stage 1 is 130 to 139 systolic or 80 to 89 diastolic mmHg; stage 2 is at or above 140 systolic or 90 diastolic mmHg.
- European and International Society of Hypertension guidance: office hypertension is at or above 140 systolic or 90 diastolic mmHg.

Out-of-office thresholds commonly used for confirmation:
- Home measurement: at or above 135/85 mmHg.
- 24-hour ambulatory average: at or above 130/80 mmHg.

## Confirmatory and supporting tests
- Validated device, correct cuff size, seated rest for at least 5 minutes, and measurement in both arms at the first visit.
- Home or ambulatory monitoring to confirm the diagnosis and detect white-coat or masked hypertension.
- Baseline tests: electrolytes, creatinine with eGFR, fasting glucose or HbA1c, lipid profile, urinalysis with albumin-to-creatinine ratio, TSH and a 12-lead ECG.
- Echocardiography when cardiac involvement is suspected.

## Differential diagnosis
- White-coat hypertension.
- Secondary causes: primary aldosteronism, renal artery stenosis, chronic kidney disease, obstructive sleep apnea, pheochromocytoma, Cushing syndrome, thyroid disease and coarctation of the aorta.
- Drug or substance effects, including NSAIDs, decongestants, stimulants and alcohol.

## Diagnostic pitfalls and red flags
- Poor technique, such as a small cuff or talking during measurement, can falsely raise readings.
- Suspect secondary hypertension with onset at a young age, resistant hypertension, hypokalemia or abrupt worsening.
- Blood pressure above 180/120 mmHg with acute target-organ damage (encephalopathy, stroke, acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary edema, aortic dissection, acute kidney injury) is a hypertensive emergency.

## Guideline basis
American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association hypertension guideline; European Society of Cardiology and European Society of Hypertension guidelines.
